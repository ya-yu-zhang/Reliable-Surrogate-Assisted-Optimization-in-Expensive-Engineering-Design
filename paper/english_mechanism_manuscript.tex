\hypertarget{abstract}{%
\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}}

We examine whether one-sided monitoring of evaluated candidates improves
objective-evaluation allocation in surrogate-assisted black-box
optimization. A candidate-independent additive correction preserves
predicted-mean ordering in exact real arithmetic under the specified
support and selection rules; floating-point outcomes are audited
separately. The main temporal prototype can therefore change decisions
through feedback exploration rather than a new ranking. We isolate
common-history calibration, common-state immediate utility, and
independent closed-loop optimization, with every diagnostic label
excluded from optimizer inputs.

The prototype fails its development optimization-benefit gate over eight
BBOB functions and dimensions 10/20. We then freeze an unchanged
mechanism validation over all 24 functions, dimensions 10/20/40,
instances 11--13, and three repeats. It completes 3,888 internal runs,
907,200 paid evaluations, and 1,990,656 additional diagnostic labels.
Relative to the development-selected fixed .1 exploration control,
independent endpoint effects are D=10: ratio 1.468, Holm p=5.36e-06;
D=20: ratio 1.258, Holm p=0.00170; D=40: ratio 1.159, Holm p=0.00170.
Nine prespecified contrasts use one Holm family; unadjusted hierarchical
intervals and every function direction are disclosed. History-policy
differences, immediate gains, improvement-declaration denominators,
localized-bound support, and final error are reported separately.
Sobol/CMA-ES provide budget-matched descriptive background evidence. The
original failed gate remains unchanged. Conclusions concern this frozen
decision rule, model, candidate process, and budget, without a coverage
theorem or general high-dimensional claim. Complete competitive,
resource, and application evidence remains unfinished.

Keywords: expensive black-box optimization; surrogate-assisted
evolutionary search; prequential residuals; post-selection error
monitoring; model management

\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\section{1 Introduction}\label{introduction}}

An objective evaluation may require a simulation, model training, or a
physical experiment. In these settings, a surrogate can help select
which candidate merits the next evaluation. The surrogate's usefulness
depends on how its predictions affect these decisions, rather than
solely on its accuracy over an arbitrary test population. A predictor
may be accurate over most proposed candidates while performing
differently on candidates favored by its own ranking. Equally, an upper
bound may rarely fail while rarely identifying useful opportunities.

We consider deterministic, single-objective optimization over a bounded
continuous domain. The objective does not drift within a run. The
surrogate, its training population, and the candidate distribution
nevertheless change as evaluated points accumulate. Historical residuals
can thus differ in both their model of origin and their prediction-time
scale. Recording them honestly supports an online empirical monitor; it
does not by itself establish exchangeability or a conformal coverage
guarantee.

Our original question is whether monitoring the error of the actually
evaluated candidates can improve objective-evaluation allocation
relative to fixed selection rules. The development protocol permits a
transition to mechanism analysis if the feedback prototype does not
improve optimization. We follow that branch rather than reinterpret a
coverage statistic as evidence of optimization efficiency.

The study separates three experiments. Common-history diagnostics
compare residual calibrators at identical frozen models and pools.
Common-state experiments compare the immediate utility of alternative
decisions without updating the history. Independent closed-loop
trajectories evaluate the resulting optimization behavior. Extra labels
used by the first two experiments are acquired only on inexpensive
analytic benchmarks, stored separately, and never provided to the
optimizer.

The contributions retained at this stage are narrowly scoped: an
ordering-equivalence result for the specified additive correction and
selection rule; an auditable implementation of prediction-before-label
residual monitoring; and empirical evidence distinguishing calibration
diagnostics, improvement declarations, and optimization utility. We do
not claim a new conformal construction, superiority to published
methods, or general high-dimensional effectiveness.

\hypertarget{related-work-and-scope-of-comparison}{%
\section{2 Related work and scope of
comparison}\label{related-work-and-scope-of-comparison}}

Online calibration has already been used in Bayesian optimization.
Deshpande, Marx, and Kuleshov {[}1{]} study calibrated predictive
distributions and online updating. Their approach differs from taking a
fixed-weight empirical quantile of historical signed residuals. Its
assumptions and guarantees cannot be imported into the present
implementation.

LOCBO {[}2{]} uses localized online conformal thresholds to calibrate a
likelihood, obtains a posterior through denoising, and uses that
posterior in optimization. Its guarantees distinguish noisy observations
from the latent objective and depend on the construction and stated
assumptions. An empirical spatial residual weight and an
effective-sample-size threshold are not equivalent substitutes.
Moreover, a constant correction to a mean ranking is not a theorem about
all distribution-calibrated acquisition functions.

Temporal conformal prediction has also been studied in
surrogate-assisted evolutionary optimization {[}3{]}. The publisher's
abstract reports temporal weighting and optimization evaluation savings.
We have verified that abstract, but have not obtained a reliable full
implementation. Consequently, the temporal residual monitor here is an
internal reference, not a reproduction of that work. The algebraic
redundancy identified below concerns our decision rule and must not be
attributed to the published method without its algorithm.

JOMI {[}4{]} addresses selection-conditional prediction through a
reference-set construction. Its theorem requires a selection rule
invariant to permutations of the calibration data and a specified
conditional exchangeability condition. Our adaptive, cross-model
residual history does not have an established justification for those
conditions. We diagnose selected-point errors but do not construct JOMI
prediction sets or claim its validity.

SADE-SS {[}5{]} studies parameter adaptation, search-space tightening,
and global/local search management. Boosting Gaussian-process
uncertainty {[}6{]} and the use of performance, spatial, and temporal
evolutionary knowledge {[}7{]} provide further context. Full text or
reliable executable details remain incomplete for several of these
methods. This manuscript therefore reports internal mechanism
comparisons and keeps the competitive evidence gap explicit.

\par\addvspace{1.5\baselineskip}\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\hypertarget{problem-information-and-decision-events}{%
\section{3 Problem, information, and decision
events}\label{problem-information-and-decision-events}}

The problem is

\[\min_{x\in\Omega}f(x),\qquad\Omega=[l,u]^D,\]

\end{minipage}\par

with a budget of \(B\) objective calls. Initialization, online startup,
repeated calls, and subsequent selected evaluations all count. No
constraints, observation noise, multiple fidelities, or multiple
objectives are included.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

At evaluation \(t\), the available labeled history is
\(\mathcal D_{t-1}=\{(x_i,y_i):i<t\}\). Before obtaining \(y_t\), we
persist the selected point, its predicted mean \(\mu_t(x_t)\), empirical
upper bound \(H_t(x_t)\), incumbent \(b_{t-1}\), prediction-time scale
\(s_t\), selection mode, and random state. The journal is flushed before
the objective is invoked. The signed prequential residual is

\[r_t=\frac{y_t-\mu_t(x_t)}{s_t}.\]

\end{minipage}\par

The scale is the population standard deviation of the current training
labels, replaced by one when it is zero. Each residual retains the
original prediction and scale. We never recalculate it with a later
model or use a training residual as if it were an online prediction
error.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

An upper-bound failure is \(e_t=\mathbb I[y_t>H_t(x_t)]\). A monitored
improvement declaration requires sufficient support and

\[H_t(x_t)<b_{t-1}-\tau_t,\qquad\tau_t=10^{-6}s_t.\]

\end{minipage}\par

It is false if \(y_t\ge b_{t-1}-\tau_t\). Every false declaration is an
upper-bound failure on that declared point. This elementary event
inclusion does not control the declaration denominator, establish a
per-declaration success probability, or imply a bound on the
false-discovery proportion. A run with no declarations has an undefined
FDP, reported as NA.

\hypertarget{search-framework-and-empirical-monitoring}{%
\section{4 Search framework and empirical
monitoring}\label{search-framework-and-empirical-monitoring}}

\hypertarget{initialization-model-and-candidates}{%
\subsection{4.1 Initialization, model, and
candidates}\label{initialization-model-and-candidates}}

We use a randomized Latin hypercube with \(n_0=2D+20\) points in a fixed
order. The first \(D+10\) points train the initial model. The next
\(D+10\) points are evaluated sequentially after prediction, forming
online startup residuals. Internal variants share this initialization
and startup process.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

Inputs are scaled to \([0,1]^D\). The regularized Gaussian RBF uses

\[d(x,x')=\|x-x'\|_2/\sqrt D,\qquad k(x,x')=\exp\{-d(x,x')^2/(2\cdot0.25^2)\},\]

\end{minipage}\par

and ridge \(10^{-6}\). Labels are standardized using the current
training subset. At most 256 points are retained: up to 128 best-valued
points followed by farthest-point sampling from the remaining archive.
Below the cap, all observations are used. This is an implementation
choice to limit dense linear-algebra costs, not an RBF complexity
advantage.

Each round proposes 256 candidates: 128 reflected DE/current-to-pbest/1
proposals with \(F=.5\), \(CR=.9\), and \(p=.2\), and 128 independently
scrambled Sobol points. The parent pool has at most 64 members,
combining elite and spatially dispersed historical points. Candidates
within \(10^{-10}\) of evaluated points or duplicates are removed and
replenished from a separate Sobol stream. Ties are resolved by the
original index. One candidate is evaluated per round. Random streams are
keyed by seed, purpose, and evaluation index; the core search does not
use the total budget.

\par\addvspace{1.5\baselineskip}\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\hypertarget{residual-upper-bounds-and-support}{%
\subsection{4.2 Residual upper bounds and
support}\label{residual-upper-bounds-and-support}}

For candidate \(x\), the empirical bound is

\[q_t(x)=\max\{0,Q^w_{.9}(r_i:i<t)\},\qquad H_t(x)=\mu_t(x)+s_tq_t(x).\]

\end{minipage}\par

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

The quantile is the smallest observed residual at which cumulative
normalized weight first reaches .9, without interpolation. We compare
four weight rules over the most recent 100 residuals: global equal
weights (G), temporal weights with a half-life of 25 evaluations (T),
spatial Gaussian weights (S), and their product (ST). Spatial bandwidth
is the twentieth-neighbor distance. The effective sample size is

\[n_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)=\frac{(\sum_iw_i)^2}{\sum_iw_i^2}.\]

\end{minipage}\par

Below 20 effective samples, ST falls back to T, S to G, and insufficient
T to G. Fewer than 20 total residuals disables bound-based improvement
selection. Support, fallback, and weighted distances are recorded. This
threshold checks weight concentration and is not a representativeness or
coverage guarantee.

\par\addvspace{1.5\baselineskip}\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\hypertarget{feedback-and-selection}{%
\subsection{4.3 Feedback and selection}\label{feedback-and-selection}}

Over at most the last 30 online predictions, let \(\bar e_t\) be the
failure fraction and \(\bar v_t\) the mean normalized margin, where
\(v_i=(H_i-\mu_i)/s_i\). Define

\[a_t=\max\left\{\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\bar e_t-.1}{.2},0,1\right),\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\bar v_t-1}{2},0,1\right)\right\}.\]

\end{minipage}\par

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

Let \(h_t\) count evaluations without significant incumbent improvement
and \(z_t=\min(h_t/20,1)\). The prototype uses

\[p_t=\operatorname{clip}(.1+.4a_t+.3z_t,.1,.8).\]

\end{minipage}\par

With fewer than 20 monitoring records, \(p_t=.5\) and the improvement
mode is disabled. Otherwise, spatial exploration with probability
\(p_t\) selects the candidate maximizing its minimum distance to the
evaluated archive. On the complementary branch, if supported improvement
candidates exist and \(a_t<.5\), the candidate with the smallest
supported empirical bound is selected; otherwise the smallest predicted
mean is selected. The coefficients are frozen development choices, not
theoretically optimal values. Higher error, width, or stagnation can
induce more exploration, but whether those evaluations benefit
optimization is an empirical question.

\hypertarget{structural-audit-and-limitations-of-event-reasoning}{%
\section{5 Structural audit and limitations of event
reasoning}\label{structural-audit-and-limitations-of-event-reasoning}}

\textbf{Ordering equivalence in exact real arithmetic.} If support is
common across candidates, \(q_t(x)=q_t\), and \(s_t\) is a common
scalar, then \(\arg\min_xH_t(x)=\arg\min_x\mu_t(x)\) under the same tie
rule. Adding the common constant \(s_tq_t\) preserves every pairwise
comparison. Checking whether an improvement candidate exists does not
change the minimizer in the studied selection rule. Thus G/T upper-bound
and mean-greedy modes select the same point on the non-exploration
branch.

If the exploration probability and random streams are also unchanged,
removing the upper mode produces the same entire trajectory by induction
over evaluations. Temporal monitoring with a fixed probability likewise
produces the corresponding fixed mean/exploration trajectory. These
configurations are retained as implementation audits, not independent
mechanism ablations. Spatial bounds can vary across candidates and need
not preserve the mean ranking. Candidate-dependent support, other
acquisition functions, evaluation-trigger decisions, or altered stopping
policies are outside this equivalence statement.

\textbf{False declarations.} If \(H_t<b_{t-1}-\tau_t\) and
\(y_t\ge b_{t-1}-\tau_t\), then \(y_t>H_t\). No distributional argument
is required. Conversely, an overall failure fraction of .1 can coexist
with a false-declaration fraction of one if all failures fall in a rare
declared subset. The supplied deterministic counterexample distinguishes
this event relation from a reliability guarantee; it is not included in
benchmark statistics.

\hypertarget{development-protocol-and-statistical-units}{%
\section{6 Development protocol and statistical
units}\label{development-protocol-and-statistical-units}}

We preselected BBOB functions 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 20, 21, and 24,
dimensions 10 and 20, instances 1--3, and three repeats per instance.
Each configuration has 144 runs. The budget is \(10D\), including
initialization. The primary prototype is T; it is not chosen after
ranking the spatial variants.

The closed-loop experiment compares 13 configurations: mean greedy,
fixed exploration probabilities .1/.3/.5/.8, temporal monitoring with
fixed .3 management, PEM-G/T/S/ST feedback, removal of the upper mode,
failure-only feedback, and stagnation-only feedback. The failure-only
variant removes both width feedback and stagnation, so it cannot
identify those two terms' separate causal contributions. Mean-greedy and
fixed strategies still compute T bounds for their monitoring journals,
but the bounds do not affect their decisions.

Common histories use mean greedy, fixed .3 mixing, and uniform selection
from the current 256-candidate pool. Pool-random is not global
independent uniform sampling, and maximum-distance exploration is not a
random history. At four frozen evaluation indices we compare G/T/S/ST
and alternative single-step choices using independently acquired pool
labels. The first checkpoint is moved beyond startup when needed:
41/50/75/100 in 10 dimensions and 61/100/150/200 in 20 dimensions. These
corrected first checkpoints are not described as 25\% budget.

Let \(E_B=\max(f_{\mathrm{best},B}-f^*,0)\) and let \(E_{n_0}\) be the
common initial error, floored at \(10^{-12}\). The endpoint is
\(\log_{10}\max(E_B/E_{n_0},10^{-12})\). For each function/instance, we
take the median over three runs, then average the three instances;
functions receive equal weight within each dimension. The paired
difference is prototype minus control, with negative values favoring the
prototype. Its base-10 exponent is a geometric summary of relative
errors, not a ratio that every run shares.

The fixed probability is selected only on development data, by
equal-weight endpoint scores over dimensions and functions. Eight
function-level paired contrasts per dimension enter Wilcoxon tests.
Twenty comparisons are jointly Holm-adjusted. Four thousand paired
hierarchical bootstrap resamples draw functions, instances, and common
run indices; each method's run median is recomputed before subtraction.
Bootstrap intervals are not multiplicity-adjusted and do not remove
uncertainty introduced by selecting the fixed control on this dataset.
No development comparison is represented as independent confirmation.

Diagnostics report pool, predicted top 10\% (26 candidates), actual
selected point, strategy-selected point, and the declared subset
separately. Failure fractions are first averaged by state, while sparse
declarations additionally disclose nonempty states, candidate counts,
and pooled failures. Candidate labels within a pool are correlated and
are not treated as independent statistical problems.

All 1,872 development runs complete without exceptions, charging 280,800
objective calls. The 432 common histories charge 64,800 calls. Their
four checkpoints yield 1,728 states and 442,368 separate diagnostic
labels. The two batches therefore execute 2,304 runs and 345,600
algorithm calls. Greedy and fixed .3 common histories repeat 288
development trajectories to capture full pools; these executions do not
increase the optimization statistical sample. Optimization statistics
use only the 1,872 development runs. Prediction-before-label ordering,
common initialization, and unchanged diagnostic-source journals pass the
audit.

PEM-T and the removed-upper-mode variant have identical trajectories in
all 144 pairs. Fixed .3 and temporal monitoring with fixed .3 management
also match in all 144 pairs. These are correspondence checks for the
structural result, not two independently effective components.

\hypertarget{discussion}{%
\section{9 Discussion}\label{discussion}}

The retained evidence has two distinct meanings. Ordering redundancy is
a deterministic property of the stated rule, supported by trajectory
audits. Increased exploration without the required development benefit
is an empirical finding about this feedback design, model, candidate
mechanism, and budget. It does not imply that all uncertainty feedback
or conformal optimization is ineffective.

Differences between pool and selected-point failures depend on history.
Fixed mixing can select either a mean-greedy candidate or a spatially
distant candidate. Labeling every such difference as greedy selection
bias obscures the exploration policy's role. Local residual weighting
may improve a diagnostic loss without establishing that the region
contains valuable optimization opportunities or that paying to evaluate
it improves future decisions.

The upper-bound margin, failure rate, declaration frequency, and final
objective must be read together. A low failure rate can be achieved by
wide bounds or nearly absent declarations. In this study, the main
prototype approximately attains its target observed failure fraction
while making very few declarations and producing worse development
endpoints. This separates empirical monitoring quality from useful
budget management.

The recorded implementation costs include fitting, candidate generation,
bound computation, selection, objective calls, and journaling.
Shared-process elapsed times are not exclusive wall-clock comparisons.
The analytic benchmark is extremely cheap and cannot establish
application acceleration. Greedy/fixed variants' recorded costs include
T monitoring that does not affect selection. Dense RBF fitting, training
truncation, and candidate generation should be evaluated separately,
alongside peak process memory, before practical time claims.

The present residual quantile provides no pointwise 90\% success
probability, selection-conditional coverage theorem, FDP/FDR guarantee,
regret bound, or global convergence theorem. Cross-model scale migration
and sparse short-budget feedback remain limitations. Development
dimensions 10 and 20 do not establish general high-dimensional
capability.

The independent mechanism validation is complete and reported separately
from development. The original optimization-improvement gate remains
failed, regardless of directions on these new instances. Sobol/CMA-ES
background evidence is descriptive; complete GP-EI/TuRBO, competitive
SAEA, exclusive resource, and application evidence remain unfinished.
Formal external comparisons cannot be inferred from partial live
batches. These gaps limit submission readiness.

The real-arithmetic ordering result does not promise identical float64
tie outcomes for arbitrary magnitudes. For example, adding \(10^{16}\)
to means {[}1,0{]} collapses their computed difference and changes the
first-index minimizer. This numerical teaching example is not a
benchmark result. Observed trajectory identities and the independent
diagnostic index audit provide empirical implementation evidence for
their respective tested scopes.

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\section{10 Conclusion}\label{conclusion}}

We implement a prequential one-sided error monitor and examine its
decision consequences through isolated diagnostics and closed-loop
development runs. Candidate-independent additive corrections preserve
the predicted-mean ordering under the specified rule. Diagnostics also
show that differences among monitoring objects depend on the history
policy. The main feedback prototype does not meet the predeclared
development optimization-benefit gate, so we retain a mechanism and
failure analysis rather than an optimization-improvement claim.
Independent mechanism validation now bounds the empirical findings
across the full suite and a third dimension. Complete
competitive/application evidence is still required; this remains a
working manuscript.
